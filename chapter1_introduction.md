# Chapter 1 — Introduction

## 1.1 The Partition of India (1947): History, Communal Violence, and Migration

In 1947, British India split into two countries: India and Pakistan. The split—driven by tensions between the Indian National Congress and the All-India Muslim League—upended millions of lives. Historians like Yasmin Khan and Ayesha Jalal have written extensively about the devastation that followed, which went far beyond political boundaries.

The violence that broke out in summer 1947 was brutal. Across the Punjab, massacres, sexual violence, and forced displacement tore through communities that had lived side by side for generations. As Khushwant Singh wrote in *Train to Pakistan*, "both sides killed. Both shot and stabbed and speared and clubbed. Both tortured. Both raped" (Singh, 1956, p. 85). historians estimate around one million people died. Government records from the period show that over a hundred thousand women were abducted, raped, or mutilated.

Roughly fourteen million people—Hindus, Muslims, and Sikhs—were displaced, making this the largest mass migration in recorded history. About 7.2 million Muslims moved from India to Pakistan, while 5.5 million Hindus and Sikhs went the other way. Trains were the main escape route, but they also became death traps; attackers targeted trains carrying civilians, massacring passengers. This forced movement shattered social bonds that had held for generations, and the trauma shaped communal relations across the subcontinent for decades.

More recent scholarship has shifted focus from grand political narratives to the experiences of ordinary people. Urvashi Butalia, Ritu Menon, and Gyanendra Pandey have challenged mainstream histories by recovering what Pandey calls the "structural trauma" of Partition—not just individual deaths but the destruction of entire communities and their social fabric. Literary accounts have been crucial here, preserving survivor stories that official histories often overlook.

## 1.2 Khushwant Singh: A Brief Biographical Outline

Khushwant Singh was born on 14 August 1915 in Hadali, a small town now in Pakistan. His family had a long tradition of military and administrative service, dating back to the courts of Maharaja Ranjit Singh and continuing through the British colonial period. He studied at Government College in Lahore, then earned a law degree from King's College, London. He worked briefly as a lawyer in Lahore before turning to journalism, contributing to *The Illustrated Weekly of India*, *The National Herald*, and *The Hindustan Times*.

His literary career spanned several decades and included novels, short stories, and non-fiction. His first short story collection, *The Mark of Vishnu and Other Stories*, came out in 1950 and established his reputation for direct prose and sharp social commentary. Other notable works include the novel *I Shall Not Hear the Nightingale* (1959), the short fiction collection *The Portrait of a Lady* (1995), the historical work *A History of the Sikhs* (1963), and his autobiography *Truth, Love, and a Little Malice* (2002). Singh wrote plainly and directly, with occasional flashes of sardonic humor. He was willing to confront social hypocrisies and religious taboos without apology.

He received many honors, including the Sahitya Akademi Award and India's Padma Vibhushan, the country's second-highest civilian award, in 2007. He died on 20 March 2014, leaving behind work that still shapes how readers understand mid-twentieth-century India.

## 1.3 *Train to Pakistan*: Publication, Setting, Plot, and Significance

Khushwant Singh's novel *Train to Pakistan* was first published in 1956 under the alternate title *Mano Majra*, named after the fictional village at the center of the story. The book is considered one of the most effective English-language accounts of Partition violence, largely because it focuses on the human cost rather than the political machinations that caused it. By setting the story in a rural Punjabi village instead of metropolitan political centers, Singh could examine how violence tore through communities bound by generations of inter-religious coexistence.

The novel takes place in Mano Majra, a small farming village near the Sutlej River on the Indo-Pakistan border. About seventy families live there, most Sikh, with a Muslim minority and one Hindu household—the moneylender Lala Ram Lal. Singh uses this village as a microcosm of pre-Partition Punjab, showing communal harmony and economic interdependence before the violence of August 1947.

The plot begins with the murder of Lala Ram Lal by the dacoit Malli and his gang. Police, acting with their usual incompetence, wrongly arrest two men: Juggut Singh (known as Jugga), a village troublemaker with a reputation for dacoity who is secretly in love with the Muslim girl Nooran; and Iqbal Singh, a social worker from Delhi who has come to Mano Majra to organize political activity. The village's peace shatters when a "ghost train" arrives from Pakistan, carrying the mutilated bodies of murdered Hindus and Sikhs. More trains follow with more bodies. Sikh refugees stream into the village, recounting atrocities against their co-religionists in Pakistan. The magistrate Hukum Chand, hoping to protect the Muslim residents, arranges for their evacuation to safety in Pakistan. Meanwhile, Sikh extremists plot to blow up the train carrying the Muslim refugees as it crosses the railway bridge. Jugga discovers that Nooran is on that train, pregnant with his child. He races to the bridge and cuts the dynamite fuse with his teeth, dying to save the passengers. The train passes safely, but the engine crushes Jugga.

The novel has four parts—"Dacoity," "Kalyug," "Mano Majra," and "Karma"—each marking a thematic phase, moving from ordinary crime through cosmic disorder to moral reckoning. Through this structure, Singh shows the catastrophic consequences of communal hatred. Jugga's self-sacrifice demonstrates that human love and moral courage can prevail over sectarian fanaticism. The novel's power lies in making the historical trauma of Partition accessible to readers while capturing the complexity of inter-community relations in rural Punjab.

## 1.4 The 1998 Film Adaptation: Pamela Rooks and Cinematic Treatment

Pamela Rooks directed the 1998 film adaptation of *Train to Pakistan*, thirty-seven years after the novel's publication. The film came out during renewed academic and popular interest in Partition history, and it was one of the few serious cinematic treatments of Partition violence to reach international audiences. Rooks, known for socially committed drama, focused on the emotional and human dimensions of the story, departing from the novel in several ways.

The film follows the novel's basic plot and themes. Both the book and the movie take place in Mano Majra, both trace Jugga and Nooran's relationship, both include the ghost train episode, and both build to the sacrifice on the railway bridge. Rooks kept the train as the central symbol—a choice that worked well in film, where the train's movement and menace could be rendered with greater immediacy than prose allows.

But the film differs from the novel in significant ways. Rooks compressed the timeline, zeroing in on the immediate crisis rather than showing the gradual buildup of tension. Some secondary characters and subplots get less attention; the novel's extended exploration of Iqbal's socialist politics and Hukum Chand's moral dilemma are trimmed to fit the format. The film gives more weight to the love story between Jugga and Nooran, and intensifies the action around the climactic bridge scene. Music features more prominently in the film than in the book, with songs like "Sanu Aa Milya Yaar Piyaariya" establishing the village's pre-violence harmony in a way the novel doesn't replicate. These changes reflect the formal and aesthetic constraints of cinema, where compression and visual storytelling necessarily reshape literary material.

## 1.5 Aim and Objectives

This dissertation examines how Khushwant Singh's *Train to Pakistan* (1956) and Pamela Rooks's 1998 film adaptation treat Partition violence. The study analyzes how the novel and the film represent communal violence, migration, and the moral dimensions of inter-religious coexistence during Partition.

The study has four objectives:

1. Examine how *Train to Pakistan* represents communal violence and mass displacement, and evaluate how the novel engages with Partition's historical trauma.
2. Analyze how Khushwant Singh constructs characters, builds themes, and uses narrative technique to present moral choices under sectarian conflict.
3. Compare the novel and the 1998 film adaptation, focusing on how cinematic choices reshape the source material.
4. Assess the significance of *Train to Pakistan* as Partition literature and its contribution to collective memory and historiographical representation of the 1947 displacement.

## 1.6 Research Method and Scope

This dissertation uses a qualitative, literary-analytical approach based on close textual analysis of the primary sources, supplemented by engagement with secondary scholarship on Partition history, historiography, and adaptive studies. The work draws on scholarly monographs, journal articles, and critical essays on Partition literature, South Asian cinema, and film adaptation theory. The study focuses on the 1956 novel and the 1998 film, with historical and critical context provided primarily through secondary sources. Comparative analysis is the main method, treating both the novel and the film as distinct but related responses to Partition.

## 1.7 Thesis Statement

Khushwant Singh's *Train to Pakistan* (1956) offers a localized, humanistic perspective on the 1947 catastrophe that contrasts with the grand political narratives dominating Partition historiography. Through Jugga and the symbolic structure of the ghost train and climactic sacrifice, the novel shows both the destructive force of communal hatred and the possibility of moral resistance through individual action. The 1998 film adaptation, while faithful to the novel's core narrative and themes, reshapes the material through cinema's formal constraints and visual possibilities, emphasizing romantic melodrama and action-revival spectacle over the novel's more extended moral and philosophical reflections. This dissertation argues that comparing novel and film illuminates not only specific adaptations of Singh's text but also broader questions about how literary and cinematic media differently engage with collective trauma, national identity, and the ethics of representing historical violence.

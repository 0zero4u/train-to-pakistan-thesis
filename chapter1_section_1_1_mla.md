# 1.1 Historical Background of Partition

The Partition of British India in 1947 resulted in one of the largest and violent mass migrations in modern history. By the summer of 1947, when the creation of Pakistan was formally announced, ten million people—Muslims, Hindus, and Sikhs—were in flight, and by the onset of the monsoon, approximately one million were dead while North India was engulfed in terror and armed conflict (Kumari, Kumari, and Singh. "Pangs of Partition in Khushwant Singh's Train to Pakistan." n.d.). The violence that accompanied Partition displaced millions along religious lines, with over twelve million people ultimately fleeing their homes ("Portrayal of Violence and Horror in Train to Pakistan." n.d.). The scale of atrocities was staggering: more than one hundred thousand women were abducted, raped, and mutilated on both sides of the border ("Portrayal of Violence and Horror in Train to Pakistan." n.d.).

The historical roots of Partition lay in the colonial period when British rule dominated the Indian subcontinent for more than four hundred years (Al-Qadhi and Pawar. "Videoing the Chaos of Evacuation in Khushwant Singh's Train to Pakistan." 2020). The British system of governance cultivated communal divisions through what historians have characterized as a "divide and rule" policy, which exacerbated tensions between religious communities (Joshi. "Politics of affect in Train to Pakistan and Tamas." 2020). The Indian independence movement, despite its unified opposition to colonial rule, could not prevent the political division of the subcontinent. Leaders including Mahatma Gandhi, who advocated for communal harmony through non-cooperation (1920–22), Civil Disobedience (1930), and the Quit India Movement (1942), found themselves unable to halt the sectarian violence that erupted upon independence (Gupta. "Study of Partition in Train to Pakistan (1956) and A Bend in the Ganges (1965)." 2014).

The Partition process itself was carried out with brutal efficiency by the departing British authorities. The province of Punjab, like Bengal, was severed in half between its Muslim-majority population and its Hindu-Sikh majority population (Kumari, Kumari, and Singh. "Pangs of Partition in Khushwant Singh's Train to Pakistan." n.d.). Communities that had coexisted for generations suddenly found themselves on opposite sides of an arbitrary border drawn by imperial cartographers. Neighbors and long-term friends turned against one another, as rumors of atrocities committed by one community inflamed violence against others, creating a vicious cycle of retaliation (Kumari, Kumari, and Singh. "Pangs of Partition in Khushwant Singh's Train to Pakistan." n.d.). Both sides engaged in killing, torture, and rape, with each accusing the other of initiating the violence (Kumari, Kumari, and Singh. "Pangs of Partition in Khushwant Singh's Train to Pakistan." n.d.).

The immediate aftermath of Partition saw not only the division of territory but also the fragmentation of families and communities. Individuals who found themselves on the wrong side of the border faced persecution, displacement, and often death. The trauma of Partition left deep imprints on the collective memory of both India and Pakistan, effects that persist in South Asian society to the present day (Kumari, Kumari, and Singh. "Pangs of Partition in Khushwant Singh's Train to Pakistan." n.d.). The events of 1947 demonstrated how the collapse of colonial authority could unleash devastating violence when coupled with communal mistrust and poorly planned territorial division.

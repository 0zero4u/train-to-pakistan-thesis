# 1.1 Historical Background of Partition

In 1947, when British India was partitioned, one of the largest and violent mass migrations in modern history followed. By the summer of that year, when Pakistan's creation became official, ten million people—Muslims, Hindus, and Sikhs—were already moving, and roughly one million had died as violence spread across North India (Kumari, Indu Kumari, and Pramod Kumar Singh 4). The violence forced millions from their homes along religious lines; ultimately, more than twelve million people fled (Unknown n. pag.). The atrocities were vast: over one hundred thousand women were abducted, raped, and mutilated on both sides of the border (Unknown n. pag.).

Partition had its roots in the colonial period, when British rule shaped the Indian subcontinent for more than four centuries (Al-Qadhi and Pawar 25). The British system of governance fostered communal divisions through what historians call "divide and rule," which deepened tensions between religious communities (Joshi 68). The Indian independence movement, though united against colonial rule, could not stop the subcontinent's political division. Leaders such as Mahatma Gandhi, who pushed for communal harmony through non-cooperation (1920–22), Civil Disobedience (1930), and the Quit India Movement (1942), found themselves powerless to prevent the sectarian violence that came with independence (Gupta 582).

The British carried out the partition process with brutal efficiency. Punjab, like Bengal, was split between its Muslim-majority areas and its Hindu-Sikh majority areas (Kumari, Indu Kumari, and Pramod Kumar Singh 5). Communities that had lived together for generations suddenly found themselves on opposite sides of a border drawn by imperial officials. Neighbors and long-time friends turned on one another as rumors of atrocities committed by one community sparked violence against others, creating a cycle of retaliation (Kumari, Indu Kumari, and Pramod Kumar Singh 4). Both sides killed, tortured, and raped, each blaming the other for starting the violence (Kumari, Indu Kumari, and Pramod Kumar Singh 3).

The immediate aftermath of Partition brought not just territorial division but also the breaking apart of families and communities. People who ended up on the wrong side of the border faced persecution, displacement, and often death. The trauma left deep marks on the collective memory of both India and Pakistan, effects that remain visible in South Asian society today (Kumari, Indu Kumari, and Pramod Kumar Singh 5). The events of 1947 showed how the collapse of colonial authority could unleash devastating violence when mixed with communal distrust and poorly planned territorial division.

## 1.6 Research Methodology and Scope

This study uses a comparative textual analysis to examine Khushwant Singh's novel *Train to Pakistan* (1956) alongside Pamela Rooks's 1998 film adaptation of the same title. Comparing the two works reveals how narrative content moves across media and what transformations occur during the shift from page to screen (Al-Qadhi and Pawar). The adaptation process involves decisions about condensation, emphasis, and visual representation, making comparison necessary for understanding both works (Goel).

The historical and literary approach places the text and film within Partition literature and cinema. Singh's novel, set in the fictional village of Mano Majra on the Indo-Pakistan border, examines the political and social dimensions of the 1947 Partition that split the Indian subcontinent into two nation-states (Al-Qadhi and Pawar). The historical approach treats Partition fiction as a way of preserving collective memory of trauma, while the literary approach examines how Singh uses narrative techniques, symbolism, and character development to convey the devastation experienced by ordinary people caught in the violence of Partition (Goel). This framework allows analysis of both the realism that the adaptation preserves and the aesthetic choices that distinguish cinematic from literary representation (Al-Qadhi and Pawar).

The study focuses on central narrative elements, thematic concerns, character portrayals, and symbolic motifs in both the novel and film. Key areas of comparison include the treatment of communal harmony and violence in Mano Majra, the love story between Jugga Singh and Nooran that crosses religious boundaries, and the symbolic importance of the train itself as a representation of British imperialism and later of Partition carnage (Goel). The study also examines how both works portray the arrival of the ghost train carrying massacred bodies, which becomes emblematic of the bloodshed that defined this historical period (Goel).

The study has limitations. It focuses on a single literary-textual pairing, which restricts generalizability to broader patterns in Partition adaptations. The textual approach prioritizes thematic and symbolic analysis over production history or reception studies. While comparative methodology shows similarities and differences between source and adaptation, it cannot fully capture the distinct aesthetic experiences that readers and viewers bring to these works. Future scholarship might expand this framework to include additional Partition narratives, cross-linguistic translations, or reception studies measuring audience responses across media.

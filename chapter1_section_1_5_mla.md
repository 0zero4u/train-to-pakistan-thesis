## Section 1.5: Aim and Objectives of the Study

This study undertakes a comparative analysis of Khushwant Singh's novel *Train to Pakistan* (1956) and Pamela Rooks's film adaptation (1998), examining representations of Partition violence, humanism, and moral conflict as depicted in both literary and cinematic media (Singh; "Portrayal of Violence"). The Partition of British India in 1947 produced one of the largest migrations in human history, with approximately twelve million people displaced and nearly half a million killed in the violence that followed across the subcontinent ("Portrayal of Violence"). This catastrophic event fundamentally altered the social, political, and cultural fabric of South Asia, constituting one of the darkest chapters in human history ("Portrayal of Violence").

The first objective of this study is to analyze how the novel *Train to Pakistan* portrays the brutal realities of Partition violence through the microcosm of Mano Majra, a fictional Punjabi village on the Indo-Pakistan border ("Portrayal of Violence"). The village, depicted as a peaceful community where Hindus, Sikhs, and Muslims had coexisted for centuries, becomes emblematic of the larger tragedy that befell the subcontinent when communal tensions erupted into widespread carnage ("Portrayal of Violence"). By examining the arrival of the "ghost train" carrying massacred bodies and the subsequent disintegration of communal harmony, the study illuminates how Singh employs literary techniques to convey the horror and devastation of the Partition ("Portrayal of Violence").

A second objective concerns the examination of humanism and moral conflict as articulated in both the novel and the film adaptation. Khushwant Singh is recognized as a champion of humanism, revealing through his narrative the inhumanity inflicted upon various sections of society while simultaneously celebrating the triumph of love, compassion, and human dignity over communal prejudice (Shahane). The character of Jugga Singh, whose act of self-sacrifice to prevent the destruction of the train represents the pinnacle of humanistic consciousness, serves as a focal point for understanding how moral choices can transcend narrow communal identities (Shahane). The study explores how Singh's portrayal of this moral awakening demonstrates that the core of human values can survive even in moments of profound crisis and tragedy (Shahane).

The third objective involves comparing the literary and cinematic treatments of the major themes inherent in the Partition narrative. The novel provides an expansive exploration of character motivations and historical context, while the film adaptation necessarily condenses these elements for cinematic impact, creating both similarities and differences in thematic representation (Goel). The study examines how the visual medium, through imagery, music, and performance, captures the realism of violence and the emotional depth of human relationships in ways that complement and diverge from the literary source (Goel). The transformation of the train from a symbol of British imperialism into an emblem of bloodshed and loss represents one such thematic element that manifests differently across the two media (Goel).

Furthermore, this study investigates how both the novel and the film address the broader implications of the Partition for human values and social cohesion. The Partition was not merely a political tragedy but fundamentally a human tragedy that shook the very foundations of belief in innate human goodness (Kumari, Indu, and Singh). By analyzing depictions of communal frenzy, mass migration, and the breakdown of traditional social bonds, the research seeks to understand how these artistic works contribute to collective memory of the Partition and its enduring legacy for South Asian society (Kumari, Indu, and Singh).

In sum, this study aims to contribute to scholarly discourse on Partition literature and cinema by providing a nuanced comparative analysis that illuminates the distinct yet complementary ways in which literary and cinematic narratives engage with themes of violence, humanism, and moral conflict. Through such an examination, the research demonstrates the enduring relevance of *Train to Pakistan* as a seminal work that continues to provoke reflection on the human cost of political division and the perennial struggle to uphold human dignity in times of crisis.

# 1.7 Thesis Statement

This dissertation argues that Khushwant Singh's *Train to Pakistan* (1956) offers a moral meditation on the human capacity for both brutality and redemption during the Partition of 1947, and that comparing the novel with Pamela Rooks's 1998 film adaptation reveals how the medium of narrative shapes the representation of historical violence and humanism. The central claim is that Singh's literary text uses interiority, retrospective moral reckoning, and the symbol of the train to construct a nuanced ethics of remembrance, while the cinematic adaptation translates these same concerns into visual spectacle that both honors and simplifies the novel's moral complexity.

*Train to Pakistan* represents Partition violence not only as political catastrophe but as a spiritual crisis that shakes the moral consciousness of ordinary people (Singh 17). Singh depicts August 1947 in Mano Majra as a moment when communal hatred erupts through the fragile veneer of interfaith coexistence, turning neighbors into executioners (Shovon 2). The novel's power lies in its refusal to sanitize this violence; as one critic observes, the text acknowledges that "both sides killed" with savage brutality, rejecting the comfortable narrative of victims and perpetrators (Singh 17). Yet within this darkness, Singh finds moments of moral resistance, particularly in the character of Juggut Singh, whose love for the Muslim woman Nooran and ultimate sacrifice to stop the massacre of Muslim refugees embodies what the text presents as the triumph of shared humanity over communal hatred ("Triumph of Human Spirit" 5).

The comparison between novel and film matters precisely because the two media negotiate moral complexity differently. The novel can explore the interiority of characters like the magistrate Hukum Chand, revealing the psychological paralysis that afflicts educated, powerful individuals confronted with collective violence ("Triumph of Human Spirit" 5). The film, by contrast, must externalize these conflicts through action and image, replacing the novel's consideration of moral weakness with dramatic confrontation. Similarly, the train itself operates differently across media: in the novel, it functions as a symbol laden with multiple significations, death, displacement, hope, while the film renders it primarily as spectacle. This dissertation argues that understanding these medial differences illuminates how collective memory of Partition gets constructed and contested, and why the novel's moral vision remains irreplaceable even as the film extends its reach to new audiences.
